    Case study · Mobile app · 2026

    StudyBuddy

    An app that helps university students get from planning to actually studying, built on interviews with seven students.

    My roleSole researcher and designer
    ContextBSc module, User-Centred System Design
    MethodsInterviews, walkthroughs, thematic analysis
    ToolsMS Teams, FigJam, Figma
    Open the Figma file ↗
    The problem

    Students plan well and still don't start

    Students juggle deadlines, crowded campuses and constant digital distraction. Plenty of tools help them plan, but few help with the moment that matters: sitting down and starting. I wanted to understand where study sessions really break down, then design for those moments.

    Research

    Seven interviews, each with a walkthrough

    I ran semi-structured interviews over MS Teams with seven undergraduates studying subjects including physics, economics, radiography, psychology and law. Each participant also walked me through a recent study session and the tools they used, thinking aloud as they went. That showed me what they actually do, not just what they say they do.

    1. Transcribe and familiarise. I reviewed transcripts alongside the recordings to catch gestures and screen-sharing.
    2. Code line by line. I tagged recurring pain points and motivations.
    3. Build an affinity diagram. In FigJam, I grouped the codes into themes that cut across participants.
    4. Turn themes into requirements. Every requirement traces back to the participants who raised it.
    What I found

    Four things get in the way

    Theme 1

    Big tasks trigger avoidance

    A task like "10 topics" felt so daunting that students avoided it, sometimes for an hour. Long, unbroken study blocks caused instant fatigue.

    Theme 2

    "Space anxiety"

    Students couldn't rely on finding a quiet desk or a working socket on campus. One described the "faffing around" that kills momentum and pushes them to study at home.

    Theme 3

    One missed session ripples

    When plans slipped, current tools made rescheduling so hard that one missed session snowballed into an overwhelming backlog.

    Theme 4

    Pretty plans take effort

    Attractive plans motivated students, but one spent ages drawing timetables by hand. Others went back to paper because apps were slow to fill in.

    Requirements

    Every feature traces back to a participant

    Break big tasks into 20–30 minute goalsP3P6P7

    Lowers the barrier to starting.

    Live campus space statusP1P3P5P6

    Filter study spaces by noise level and working sockets before leaving home.

    Automatic reschedulingP3P7

    Recalculates the plan when a session is missed, so guilt doesn't build into a backlog.

    Low-effort visual themesP1P6

    Keeps the motivation of a beautiful plan without the drawing time.

    Opt-in focus mode that blocks appsP2P3P5

    Locks distracting social media during a session.

    Synthesis

    Meet Zainab, the last-minute power user

    Zainab is a composite of the patterns I found: she works best when a deadline creates urgency, needs the right environment to focus, and finds complex apps clunky.

    I left out demographic details that didn't affect her needs, so the persona stays a tool for design decisions rather than a stereotype.

    "I just need a quiet organised space with natural light where I can lock in."

    Design

    One journey, six screens

    I mapped a single journey, from seeing a deadline to finishing a session and recovering the week's plan, then designed six annotated wireframes along it. Each annotation links a design choice to a research finding or a usability principle.

    Evaluation plan

    How I'd test it

    I planned a moderated usability test with five students who match Zainab's profile, in a usability lab with screen and gesture recording. I'd measure task completion, time on task and errors, alongside think-aloud comments and a System Usability Scale questionnaire.

    Task 1

    Plan

    Break a 30% essay into 20-minute chunks for a low-energy day.

    Success: a plan within 60 seconds.

    Task 2

    Find a space

    Find a silent library desk with a working socket.

    Success: correct filters and a building with free seats.

    Task 3

    Recover

    Fit yesterday's missed task into the coming week.

    Success: confirms the new schedule without hesitating.

    Using AI responsibly

    Where AI helped, and where I checked it

    I used Google Gemini to draft anonymised interview summaries, then checked each one against the original transcript and corrected it. I used Figma AI to generate background images for the theme and audio options on two screens. The analysis and design were my own.

    Next step

    What I'd do next

    You can explore the persona, journey map, user flow and all six wireframes in the original Figma file.

    The usability test is planned but not yet run. Next, I'd build a clickable high-fidelity prototype, test it with five students, and use the results to refine the task breakdown and rescheduling screens, which carry the riskiest assumptions.

    Case study · Nuffield Research Placement · 2021

    Patient-Counsellor Matching App

    How could AI make society safer? My answer: help people find a counsellor who understands their background.

    My roleSole author of the report
    HostSynoptix, a systems engineering consultancy
    Reviewed byA project engineer and a systems engineer
    OutcomeGold CREST Award
    The problem

    Long waits, and feeling misunderstood

    On my Nuffield Research Placement, Synoptix set the question "How could AI be used to increase safety in our society?" I chose mental health. My research pointed to two problems: long waiting lists for NHS talking therapies, and patients, particularly people of colour, people from low-income backgrounds and LGBTQ+ people, feeling that their counsellor didn't understand their background. When advice doesn't fit someone's culture or circumstances, it may not help, and misunderstanding can even contribute to misdiagnosis.

    The concept

    Match patients to counsellors they can relate to

    Patients answer a questionnaire about their needs and their preferences for a counsellor, such as gender, ethnicity or availability. Verified counsellors list their experience and specialisms. The app then suggests several ranked matches, so a patient still has options if their best match is busy. The patient always makes first contact and books a taster session before committing.

    • Privacy by design: patients' data is visible only to them and the counsellor they choose to share it with, and the system must comply with GDPR and encrypt all data.
    • Safety: a human moderator checks every counsellor's qualifications before they can join.
    • Accessibility: options such as text-to-speech for users who struggle to see the screen.
    Systems engineering approach

    From use cases to requirements

    I followed a systems engineering process: use case diagrams for both patients and counsellors, then functional and non-functional requirements written to be SMART (specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and timely), for example "The system shall provide at least five potential counsellors" and "The system shall notify a counsellor when a patient has matched with them." I also reviewed similar services, including Kooth, the Samaritans and Childline, to find the gap my concept would fill.

    For the matching itself, I designed a machine learning flow: split the data into training and test sets, use classification for categories like ethnicity and regression for continuous values like session length, use natural language processing to pick out keywords from free text (such as "trauma"), and evaluate the model with a confusion matrix or RMSE. Patient feedback would then reinforce good matches over time.

    Risks

    What could go wrong

    • Too few counsellors to meet some preferences, which ranked matches help with.
    • Integrating with NHS infrastructure would be a long process involving consent and data sharing.
    • People without a smartphone, or who struggle with technology, could be excluded.
    Looking back

    Where this led

    I wrote this at 17, and it's where my interest in safe, trustworthy AI in healthcare started. Today I'd begin with research rather than a solution: interviewing patients and counsellors before writing requirements. That's exactly the approach I now use, and it's why I'm studying Human-AI Interaction and HCI for Health at UCL.

    Case study · Final year project · 2025–26

    Student Circus

    A peer-to-peer mentoring platform that connects international students with verified alumni for honest career and university advice.

    My roleSole designer and full-stack developer
    ClientStudent Circus
    StackVue.js 3, Laravel 11, SQLite
    ModuleIndividual Project (71.5)
    The problem

    An information gap

    International students often don't know anyone who has studied their course or worked in their target industry in the UK. Student Circus wanted a way to connect them with alumni mentors who could give career guidance and an honest view of university life, while making sure every mentor was who they said they were.

    Sketching first

    From notebook to wireframes

    I started on paper, sketching the global navigation, a student home page, the mentor search, mentor profiles, booking checkout, mentor sign-up and the mentor dashboard. Then I turned the sketches into digital wireframes, adding a verification flow for mentors and an admin dashboard.

    What I built

    A working product

    Mentor directory

    Students search and filter mentors by expertise or university, and can save favourites.

    Booking that fits real life

    Sessions book in 15-minute steps within professional hours (8am to 6pm), and confirmed meetings sync to the mentor's Google Calendar.

    Verified mentors

    Mentors upload ID during onboarding, and an admin approves or rejects them before they appear to students.

    Feedback and ratings

    After a session, students leave a review, and the mentor's average rating updates straight away.

    Behind it is a Laravel 11 REST API with a relational SQLite database (users, universities, mentor and student profiles, availability, meetings, reviews and favourites) and a Vue.js 3 front end, with separate dashboards for students, mentors and admins.

    What it shows

    Why it matters for the work I want to do

    This project was about trust as much as code: making sure students could trust who they were talking to, and making booking simple for busy mentors. It's the same thinking I bring to healthcare and AI, where people need to know they can rely on a system.
