Case study · Mobile app · 2026

StudyBuddy

An app that helps university students get from planning to actually studying, built on interviews with seven students.

My roleSole researcher and designer
ContextBSc module, User-Centred System Design
MethodsInterviews, walkthroughs, thematic analysis
ToolsMS Teams, FigJam, Figma
The problem

Students plan well and still don't start

Students juggle deadlines, crowded campuses and constant digital distraction. Plenty of tools help them plan, but few help with the moment that matters: sitting down and starting. I wanted to understand where study sessions really break down, then design for those moments.

Research

Seven interviews, each with a walkthrough

I ran semi-structured interviews over MS Teams with seven undergraduates from different subjects, including physics, economics, radiography, psychology and law. In each session, participants also walked me through a recent study session and the tools they used, thinking aloud as they went. That gave me evidence of what they actually do, not just what they say they do.

  1. Transcribe and familiarise. I reviewed transcripts alongside the recordings to catch gestures and screen-sharing.
  2. Code line by line. I tagged recurring pain points and motivations.
  3. Build an affinity diagram. In FigJam, I grouped the codes into themes that cut across participants.
  4. Turn themes into requirements. Every requirement traces back to the participants who raised it.
What I found

Four things get in the way

Theme 1

Big tasks trigger avoidance

A task like "10 topics" felt so daunting that students avoided it, sometimes for an hour. Long, unbroken study blocks caused instant fatigue.

Theme 2

"Space anxiety"

Students couldn't rely on finding a quiet desk or a working socket on campus. One described the faffing around that kills momentum and pushes them to study at home.

Theme 3

One missed session ripples

When plans slipped, current tools made rescheduling so hard that one missed session snowballed into an overwhelming backlog.

Theme 4

Pretty plans take effort

Students found attractive plans motivating, but one spent ages drawing timetables by hand. Others went back to paper because apps were slow to fill in.

Requirements

Synthesis

Meet Zainab, the last-minute power user

Zainab is a composite of the behaviour patterns I found: she works best when a deadline creates urgency, needs the right environment to focus, and finds complex apps clunky.

I left out demographic details that didn't affect her needs, so the persona stays a tool for design decisions rather than a stereotype.

"I just need a quiet organised space with natural light where I can lock in."

Design

One journey, six screens

I mapped a single journey, from seeing a deadline to finishing a session and recovering the week's plan, then designed six annotated wireframes along it. Each annotation links a design choice to a research finding or a usability principle.

Evaluation plan

How I'd test it

I planned a moderated usability test with five students who match Zainab's profile, run in a usability lab with screen and gesture recording. I'd measure task completion, time on task and errors, alongside think-aloud comments and a System Usability Scale questionnaire.

Task 1

Plan

Break a 30% essay into 20-minute chunks for a low-energy day.

Success: a plan within 60 seconds.

Task 2

Find a space

Find a silent library desk with a working socket.

Success: correct filters and a building with free seats.

Task 3

Recover

Fit yesterday's missed task into the coming week.

Success: confirms the new schedule without hesitating.

Using AI responsibly

Where AI helped, and where I checked it

I used Google Gemini to draft anonymised interview summaries, then checked each one against the original transcript and corrected it. I used Figma AI to generate the background images for the theme and audio options on two screens. Everything else, including the analysis, I did by hand.

Next step

What I'd do next

The usability test above is planned but not yet run. The next step is to build a clickable high-fidelity prototype, run it with five students, and use the results to refine the task breakdown and rescheduling screens, which carry the riskiest assumptions.
